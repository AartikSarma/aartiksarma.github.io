I study why the same treatment works differently in different people.

I'm a pulmonary and critical care physician at UCSF. I look after people in the intensive care unit, where that question isn't academic — the treatments we have are blunt, and we usually can't tell in advance which patients they will help.

Critical illness gets diagnosed from what you can see at the bedside — an oxygen level, a chest X-ray, a blood pressure that won't hold. Those definitions were built to work at 3 a.m. anywhere in the world, and they do. But they put patients with almost nothing biologically in common into the same box. A treatment that helps one group and harms another then comes out of a trial looking like it does nothing at all, which is part of why so much of what works in the lab fails in large clinical trials.

So my lab reads the biology directly. We sequence RNA from fluid suctioned out of the breathing tubes of ventilated patients — a sample collected as part of routine care and usually thrown away — and use it to find the distinctions the bedside definitions miss.

A few things that came out of that:

  • Blood tests split ARDS into two subtypes that respond differently to treatment, but nobody had checked whether the split was real inside the lung. It is — one subtype shows far more interferon signaling and T-cell activity in the airway itself.
  • The formula setting how large a breath every ventilated patient receives was derived from height and sex in the 1990s. Across 21,000 patients, it fits some people much worse than others — and the worse the fit, the higher the odds of dying. Algorithmic bias, thirty years before machine learning.
  • At the peak of the pandemic, "cytokine storm" was the working theory for COVID-19 lung injury. Airway sequencing showed the opposite: less inflammatory gene expression than in other kinds of ARDS, not more.

Patient data mostly can't leave the hospital that holds it, so a growing share of my work is infrastructure for asking questions across many hospitals at once — along with open tools for RNA-seq workflows and Bayesian re-analysis of clinical trials. If you work on that problem in any field, I'd like to hear from you.

I also talk about this publicly now and then — usually about where machine learning genuinely helps in medicine, and where it's the wrong tool for the job.
